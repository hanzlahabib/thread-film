# Why this works

The lessons behind the pattern, so you can defend a choice when a film is being reviewed.

## Isolated callouts read as decoration
A film where each feature gets its own screen, its own zoom, its own spring callout and then a cut looks busy but says little. Each effect starts and ends on its own screen, so nothing explains how one thing leads to another. Viewers see motion graphics, not a product.

## One connected line shows cause and effect
A single line that leaves one element and lands on the next says "this leads to that" without a word. The viewer follows it the way they follow a sentence. Because the line never breaks, the film cannot turn into a slideshow.

## Go deeper per screen
One touch per feature is too shallow. Three or four stops per screen, in the order a user acts, show the steps inside a feature: that is what makes a film feel detailed and credible rather than a list of names.

## Carry objects across screens
A chip that rides the head of the line and settles into the next screen answers "what happened to the thing I just saw?". A lead becomes a row, a sent message becomes a conversation, a deal value becomes a number in a report. The carried object is what turns separate screens into one story.

## Arrival causes the next thing
Rings, labels and counters fire when the line lands, never before. The line is the clock of the film; because everything is triggered by it, nothing floats free and the eye always knows where to look.

## Real screens
Real captures with demo data are believable in a way mockups are not, and they cannot promise what the product does not do.

## Checklist for a reviewer
- Could any label be removed without the line noticing? If so, it is decoration.
- Does every screen change carry an object?
- Is there a point where the line stops at a cut?
